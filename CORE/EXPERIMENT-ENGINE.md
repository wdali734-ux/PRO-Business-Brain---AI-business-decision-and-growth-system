# EXPERIMENT ENGINE

## Purpose

The Experiment Engine converts uncertainty into measurable learning through controlled, low-cost tests.

Its purpose is to prevent the business from making large commitments based on untested assumptions.

Core rule:

> When uncertainty is high and testing is cheap, test before scaling.

---

# 1. EXPERIMENT MISSION

An experiment should answer a meaningful question.

It should not exist merely to create activity.

Every experiment must connect:

> ASSUMPTION → HYPOTHESIS → TEST → METRIC → THRESHOLD → DECISION

---

# 2. WHEN TO EXPERIMENT

Prefer experimentation when:

- uncertainty is high
- the assumption materially affects the decision
- testing is reasonably cheap
- the result can change the strategy
- the decision is expensive
- the decision is difficult to reverse
- real-world behavior is more informative than opinion

Do not experiment when:

- the answer is already sufficiently known
- the test cannot change the decision
- the test costs more than the information is worth
- the variable is not controllable
- the experiment is too weak to produce useful evidence

---

# 3. EXPERIMENT DEFINITION

Every experiment should define:

### Question

What are we trying to learn?

### Assumption

What must be true for the strategy to work?

### Hypothesis

What specifically do we predict?

### Test

What will we do?

### Metric

What will we measure?

### Threshold

What result counts as success?

### Failure condition

What result causes rejection or modification?

### Time window

How long will the test run?

### Decision

What will we do with the result?

---

# 4. HYPOTHESIS FORMAT

Use:

> If [action or condition], then [expected outcome], because [mechanism].

Example:

> If we target small e-commerce businesses with a simplified offer, then qualified sales calls will increase because the message addresses a specific pain point.

A hypothesis should be:

- specific
- measurable
- falsifiable
- connected to a mechanism

Avoid vague hypotheses.

Bad:

> Customers will like this.

Better:

> At least 10% of qualified prospects will request a demo after seeing the offer.

---

# 5. ASSUMPTION PRIORITY

Not every assumption deserves a test.

Prioritize assumptions using:

> Impact × Uncertainty × Testability

High-impact + high-uncertainty + cheap-to-test assumptions should be tested first.

---

# 6. RISKEST ASSUMPTION FIRST

When a business model contains multiple assumptions, identify the assumption most likely to kill the model if false.

Examples:

- customers will pay
- acquisition is affordable
- retention is sufficient
- margins are viable
- delivery is scalable
- the problem is painful enough
- the founder/team can execute

Test the riskiest important assumption before optimizing secondary details.

---

# 7. MINIMUM VIABLE EXPERIMENT

Design the smallest experiment capable of producing useful evidence.

Avoid:

- building full products before demand validation
- large ad budgets before message validation
- hiring before workload validation
- nationwide launches before local validation
- complex automation before manual validation

Prefer:

- prototype
- manual service
- landing page
- direct outreach
- paid pilot
- pre-order
- small ad test
- limited launch

The goal is not to build the final system.

The goal is to learn whether the critical assumption is true.

---

# 8. EXPERIMENT LADDER

Use progressively stronger evidence.

### Level 1: Opinion

Ask customers what they think.

Useful for discovering language and problems.

Weak evidence of future behavior.

### Level 2: Intent

Waitlists, signups, requests, commitments.

Stronger than opinions.

Still weaker than payment.

### Level 3: Behavior

Clicks, demos, trials, usage.

Shows actual behavior.

### Level 4: Payment

Deposits, pre-orders, purchases.

Strong evidence of willingness to pay.

### Level 5: Repeat Behavior

Repeat purchases, retention, recurring usage.

Stronger evidence of durable value.

### Level 6: Economics

Positive unit economics at meaningful scale.

Strongest validation for a scalable business model.

---

# 9. TEST DESIGN

A good experiment should change as few variables as practical.

Define:

- target population
- variable being tested
- control or baseline
- test condition
- sample
- duration
- metric
- threshold
- decision rule

Avoid changing many variables simultaneously unless the purpose is only broad validation.

---

# 10. BASELINE

Whenever practical, establish a baseline.

Compare:

> Before vs After

or:

> Control vs Test

or:

> Old Offer vs New Offer

Without a baseline, improvement can be difficult to interpret.

---

# 11. CONTROL GROUP

When practical, use a control.

Example:

Control:

> Existing offer

Test:

> New offer

This helps isolate the effect of the change.

A control is not always possible.

When unavailable, clearly state the limitation.

---

# 12. SAMPLE SIZE

Do not overinterpret tiny samples.

A result from:

> 3 customers

should not be treated like a result from:

> 300 customers.

When sample sizes are small:

- lower confidence
- avoid precise claims
- treat results as directional
- consider repeating the test

Small experiments are useful for learning.

They are not automatically statistically conclusive.

---

# 13. TEST DURATION

Run the experiment long enough to capture the relevant behavior.

Consider:

- sales cycle
- purchase frequency
- seasonality
- traffic volume
- learning curve
- operational delay

Do not stop immediately because of one early result unless the signal is overwhelming.

Do not continue indefinitely after the decision-relevant evidence is already clear.

---

# 14. SUCCESS THRESHOLD

Define success before observing the result.

Example:

> Proceed if conversion exceeds 5%.

or:

> Continue if CAC remains below $30.

or:

> Scale if at least 20 customers complete the paid pilot and retention exceeds 60%.

Thresholds should connect to economics or strategic objectives.

---

# 15. DECISION RULE

Every meaningful experiment should have a predefined decision rule.

### GREEN

Result exceeds the success threshold.

→ Continue or scale cautiously.

### YELLOW

Result is promising but inconclusive.

→ Improve the test or collect more evidence.

### RED

Result fails the minimum threshold.

→ Reject, modify, or investigate a different hypothesis.

Do not move the goalposts after seeing the result merely to preserve the original strategy.

---

# 16. ECONOMIC THRESHOLDS

Vanity metrics are not enough.

Whenever possible connect the experiment to:

- revenue
- gross margin
- contribution margin
- CAC
- LTV
- payback period
- retention
- conversion
- capacity
- cash flow

Example:

A campaign producing cheap leads may still be a failure if the leads do not convert into profitable customers.

---

# 17. EXPERIMENT QUALITY

Evaluate experiments using:

### Relevance

Does the experiment test an important assumption?

### Isolation

Can we reasonably attribute the result to the variable being tested?

### Measurement

Is the metric reliable?

### Threshold

Was success defined before the result?

### Speed

Can we learn quickly?

### Cost

Is the experiment cheap relative to the decision?

### Actionability

Will the result change what we do?

A beautiful experiment that cannot change a decision is strategically weak.

---

# 18. INFORMATION VALUE

The value of an experiment depends on:

> Probability of changing the decision × value of changing the decision

An experiment is especially valuable when:

- the current decision is uncertain
- the downside of being wrong is large
- the experiment is cheap
- the result is likely to be informative

---

# 19. EXPERIMENT COST

Track total experiment cost.

Include:

- money
- time
- team capacity
- opportunity cost
- customer goodwill
- technical effort
- operational disruption

A cheap experiment is not necessarily cheap if it consumes critical founder time.

---

# 20. REVERSIBILITY

Prefer experiments that are easy to stop.

Examples:

- small ad budget
- limited pilot
- temporary offer
- manual process
- small customer cohort

Avoid irreversible commitments before critical assumptions are validated.

---

# 21. CUSTOMER EXPERIMENTS

For customer validation, prioritize behavior.

Weak:

> "Would you buy this?"

Stronger:

> "Would you pay $X for this?"

Stronger:

> "Here is the offer. Would you like to purchase it?"

Strongest practical validation:

> Customer actually pays and receives value.

---

# 22. PRICING EXPERIMENTS

When testing price, measure more than conversion.

Track:

- conversion
- revenue per visitor
- gross margin
- contribution margin
- refund rate
- retention
- customer quality

A lower price may increase conversion while reducing total profit.

The best price is not necessarily the price with the highest conversion.

---

# 23. OFFER EXPERIMENTS

When testing an offer, change one major variable at a time when practical:

- target customer
- promise
- mechanism
- deliverables
- guarantee
- price
- urgency
- positioning

Measure the outcome closest to the actual business objective.

---

# 24. MARKETING EXPERIMENTS

Possible variables:

- audience
- hook
- message
- creative
- channel
- offer
- landing page
- call to action

Do not optimize click-through rate while ignoring downstream customer quality.

---

# 25. SALES EXPERIMENTS

Possible variables:

- lead qualification
- script
- offer presentation
- objection handling
- follow-up
- pricing
- sales channel

Measure:

- contact rate
- qualification rate
- close rate
- revenue
- sales cycle
- refund rate
- customer quality

---

# 26. PRODUCT EXPERIMENTS

Test:

- problem severity
- feature demand
- usability
- activation
- engagement
- retention
- willingness to pay

Do not confuse feature usage with customer value.

A frequently used feature can still fail to produce meaningful outcomes.

---

# 27. RETENTION EXPERIMENTS

When retention is weak, test:

- onboarding
- time to value
- product quality
- customer success
- usage frequency
- pricing
- support
- customer selection

Do not automatically assume churn is a product problem.

Customer quality and acquisition source can also drive retention.

---

# 28. MULTIVARIATE WARNING

Testing many variables simultaneously can make results difficult to interpret.

If:

- price changes
- offer changes
- audience changes
- landing page changes

all at once, a positive or negative result may not reveal which change caused it.

Use broader bundled tests only when speed matters more than precise attribution.

---

# 29. EXPERIMENT SEQUENCING

Run experiments in logical order.

Prefer:

1. Validate demand.
2. Validate willingness to pay.
3. Validate acquisition.
4. Validate conversion.
5. Validate delivery.
6. Validate retention.
7. Validate economics.
8. Scale.

Do not optimize downstream systems before upstream assumptions are sufficiently validated.

---

# 30. EXPERIMENT PORTFOLIO

When multiple experiments are available, prioritize by:

> Expected learning value ÷ cost

Then adjust for:

- strategic importance
- downside risk
- speed
- reversibility

Run the highest-value learning experiments first.

---

# 31. FAILURE IS INFORMATION

A failed experiment is not automatically wasted effort.

Ask:

- What assumption failed?
- Was the test valid?
- Was the threshold appropriate?
- Was the sample sufficient?
- Did another variable contaminate the result?
- What should change?
- Should the hypothesis be rejected or modified?

Do not label a test a failure if the experiment itself was poorly designed.

---

# 32. FAILED EXPERIMENT PROTOCOL

If the result is negative:

1. Verify the data.
2. Verify the experiment was executed correctly.
3. Check for confounding variables.
4. Compare against the predefined threshold.
5. Determine which assumption failed.
6. Decide whether to:
   - reject
   - modify
   - retest
   - gather more evidence
7. Update the business model or strategy.

Never automatically "try harder" without understanding why the test failed.

---

# 33. EXPERIMENT BIAS

Watch for:

- stopping early because results look exciting
- continuing because results are disappointing
- changing the threshold after the result
- cherry-picking customers
- excluding inconvenient data
- testing only friendly users
- testing only the best-performing segment
- changing multiple variables
- confusing correlation with causation
- declaring success from tiny samples
- interpreting random variation as a trend

---

# 34. LEARNING LOG

Every significant experiment should produce a record:

- date
- question
- hypothesis
- assumption
- test
- target population
- sample
- metric
- baseline
- threshold
- result
- confidence
- interpretation
- decision
- next experiment

This creates organizational memory.

---

# 35. EXPERIMENT MEMORY

Do not repeatedly test the same question without learning from previous tests.

Store:

> What we believed → what we tested → what happened → what changed.

Previous evidence should update future hypotheses.

---

# 36. AI EXPERIMENT PROTOCOL

When operating as an AI business advisor:

1. Identify the decision that needs information.
2. Identify the highest-impact uncertainty.
3. Convert it into a falsifiable hypothesis.
4. Define the smallest useful test.
5. Define the metric.
6. Define the success threshold.
7. Define the failure threshold.
8. Estimate cost.
9. Define duration.
10. Define the decision rule.
11. Run or recommend the test.
12. Analyze the result.
13. Update the relevant assumptions.
14. Recommend the next action.

The AI must not recommend experiments merely to appear rigorous.

Every experiment must have a purpose.

---

---

# 38. SCALE GATE

Do not scale simply because an experiment produced a positive result.

Before scaling, verify:

- result is economically meaningful
- evidence is sufficiently reliable
- result is repeatable enough
- operational capacity exists
- downside is manageable
- customer quality is acceptable
- unit economics remain viable

Scaling amplifies both good systems and bad systems.

---

# 39. REPLICATION

When a result matters, attempt to reproduce it.

Ask:

> Does the result survive another reasonable test?

Replication increases confidence.

A single positive result should usually be treated as evidence, not permanent truth.

---

# 40. EXPERIMENT → DECISION LOOP

The complete loop is:

QUESTION

↓

HYPOTHESIS

↓

TEST

↓

MEASURE

↓

COMPARE WITH THRESHOLD

↓

DECIDE

↓

UPDATE ASSUMPTION

↓

RUN NEXT TEST

The system must continuously convert uncertainty into learning.

---

# 41. EXPERIMENT ENGINE + TRUTH ENGINE

The Truth Engine determines:

> What do we actually know?

The Experiment Engine determines:

> What is the cheapest way to learn what we do not know?

Together:

> Evidence → uncertainty → test → evidence update.

---

# 42. EXPERIMENT ENGINE + DECISION ENGINE

The Decision Engine determines:

> What should we choose?

The Experiment Engine determines:

> What should we learn before choosing?

Therefore:

If uncertainty is decision-critical:

> Experiment first.

If uncertainty is decision-irrelevant:

> Decide and move.

---

# 43. EXPERIMENT ENGINE + CRITIC ENGINE

The Critic Engine asks:

> Why might this experiment give us the wrong conclusion?

Challenge:

- sample
- metric
- threshold
- execution
- attribution
- selection bias
- timing
- external variables

A weak experiment can create false confidence.

---

# 44. EXPERIMENT ENGINE + HORMOZI

Hormozi frameworks may suggest:

- offer tests
- pricing tests
- guarantee tests
- acquisition tests
- sales tests
- retention tests

The Experiment Engine determines:

- what assumption is being tested
- how to test it
- what metric matters
- what threshold determines success
- what happens next

Frameworks generate candidate strategies.

Experiments determine whether those strategies work in the specific business.

---

# 45. FINAL EXPERIMENT RULE

Never run an experiment simply because experimentation sounds intelligent.

Run an experiment when it can materially improve a decision.

The ideal experiment is:

- cheap
- fast
- focused
- measurable
- falsifiable
- reversible
- economically relevant
- decision-changing

The goal is not more experiments.

The goal is faster learning with less wasted capital.

> Test the riskiest important assumption before scaling the strategy.
