# DECISION MATRIX

## Purpose

The Decision Matrix converts competing options, hypotheses, and strategies into a transparent decision.

Its purpose is not to manufacture mathematical certainty.

Its purpose is to make tradeoffs visible, reduce bias, and identify which option deserves action or testing.

Core rule:

> Do not choose the option that feels best. Choose the option that survives structured comparison.

---

# 1. WHEN TO USE

Use the Decision Matrix when:

- multiple viable options exist
- competing hypotheses require prioritization
- tradeoffs are significant
- resources are limited
- uncertainty is meaningful
- the decision is difficult to reverse
- intuition produces conflicting answers
- stakeholders disagree
- the cost of choosing incorrectly is high

Do not use it for trivial decisions.

---

# 2. DECISION INPUT

Before comparing options, define:

### Decision

What must be decided?

### Desired outcome

What are we trying to achieve?

### Constraints

What cannot be changed?

Examples:

- budget
- time
- team
- technology
- legal constraints
- capacity
- geography

### Deadline

When must the decision be made?

### Reversibility

Can the decision easily be reversed?

### Cost of being wrong

What happens if we choose incorrectly?

---

# 3. DEFINE THE OPTIONS

Create at least three meaningful alternatives when practical.

Include:

### Option A: Preferred strategy

The option currently believed to be strongest.

### Option B: Alternative strategy

A credible competing approach.

### Option C: Minimum viable strategy

The simplest, cheapest, or fastest viable option.

### Option D: Status quo

What happens if we do nothing?

### Option E: Experimental option

A small test before committing to a full strategy.

Do not create fake options merely to reach a number.

---

# 4. FALSE CHOICE CHECK

Before scoring options ask:

> Are these actually different decisions?

Sometimes apparent alternatives can be combined.

Example:

A:

> Run paid ads.

B:

> Improve organic content.

The real strategy may be:

> Improve conversion first, then test both acquisition channels.

Do not force mutually exclusive choices when sequencing can solve the problem.

---

# 5. DECISION CRITERIA

Define the criteria before scoring.

Typical criteria include:

- expected impact
- probability of success
- cost
- speed
- risk
- reversibility
- strategic fit
- customer value
- operational complexity
- scalability
- margin
- learning value
- resource requirements

Use only criteria relevant to the decision.

More criteria do not automatically improve the decision.

---

# 6. CRITERIA WEIGHTING

Assign each criterion a weight according to importance.

Example:

| Criterion | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Expected impact | 30% |
| Probability | 25% |
| Cost | 15% |
| Speed | 10% |
| Risk | 10% |
| Learning value | 10% |

Weights should total:

> 100%

Do not manipulate weights simply to make the preferred option win.

---

# 7. SCORING

Score each option against each criterion.

Recommended scale:

> 1 = Very poor  
> 2 = Poor  
> 3 = Acceptable  
> 4 = Strong  
> 5 = Excellent

Scores should be evidence-based whenever possible.

A score based entirely on intuition must be labeled as an assumption.

---

# 8. WEIGHTED SCORE

For each option:

> Weighted Score = Σ(Criterion Weight × Option Score)

Example:

Option A:

| Criterion | Weight | Score | Weighted |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Impact | 30% | 5 | 1.50 |
| Probability | 25% | 4 | 1.00 |
| Cost | 15% | 3 | 0.45 |
| Speed | 10% | 4 | 0.40 |
| Risk | 10% | 3 | 0.30 |
| Learning | 10% | 5 | 0.50 |

Total:

> 4.15 / 5

The calculation makes the reasoning visible.

It does not make the result objectively true.

---

# 9. SCORE QUALITY

Every important score should have a basis.

Classify the basis as:

- measured data
- external evidence
- historical performance
- benchmark
- experiment
- expert judgment
- assumption

If a score depends heavily on assumptions, confidence in the final matrix should decrease.

---

# 10. EXPECTED VALUE

Weighted scoring is not enough when outcomes have very different economic consequences.

Use expected value when practical:

> Expected Value = Probability × Outcome Value − Cost

Example:

Option A:

40% probability of generating $20,000 profit.

Expected benefit:

> $8,000

If the test costs $1,000:

Expected net value:

> $7,000

Expected value should be treated as a decision aid, not a prediction of what will definitely happen.

---

# 11. ASYMMETRIC UPSIDE AND DOWNSIDE

Do not evaluate only average outcomes.

Consider:

### Best case

What happens if everything works?

### Base case

What is the most reasonable outcome?

### Worst case

What happens if the strategy fails?

### Downside severity

How damaging is failure?

### Recovery

Can the business recover?

An option with slightly lower expected value may be preferable if its downside is dramatically smaller.

---

# 12. REVERSIBILITY

Classify decisions:

### Reversible

Can be undone cheaply.

### Partially reversible

Can be changed, but with meaningful cost.

### Irreversible

Difficult or expensive to reverse.

For reversible decisions:

> Decide faster and learn through execution.

For irreversible decisions:

> Research more before committing.

---

# 13. INFORMATION VALUE

Sometimes the best option is not an immediate strategy.

It is an experiment that creates information.

Ask:

> Which action teaches us the most at the lowest cost?

Example:

Instead of investing $20,000 into a new product:

> Spend $500 testing whether customers will actually pay.

The experiment may have lower immediate upside but much higher information value.

---

# 14. HYPOTHESIS SELECTION

When choosing between competing hypotheses, score them on:

- potential impact
- probability
- uncertainty
- testability
- cost of testing
- time to learn

A strong first hypothesis is often:

> High impact + high uncertainty + cheap test.

Do not automatically choose the hypothesis with the highest probability.

A low-probability hypothesis can deserve testing if its impact is enormous and the test is cheap.

---

# 15. DECISION UNDER UNCERTAINTY

Do not force certainty where none exists.

When evidence is incomplete:

1. identify the uncertainty
2. estimate its decision impact
3. compare the cost of resolving it
4. decide whether to research, experiment, or act
5. choose the smallest commitment consistent with the evidence

---

# 16. ROBUSTNESS TEST

After calculating the result, ask:

> Would the decision remain the same if reasonable assumptions changed?

Change important assumptions.

Examples:

- probability ±20%
- cost +30%
- conversion rate −20%
- customer acquisition cost +30%
- delivery time +25%

If the winner changes easily, the decision is fragile.

If the winner remains strong across reasonable scenarios, confidence increases.

---

# 17. SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS

Identify which variables have the greatest influence on the decision.

Ask:

> Which assumption, if changed, would most likely change the winner?

That variable deserves additional research or experimentation.

Do not waste effort refining variables that cannot change the decision.

---

# 18. STRONGEST COUNTERARGUMENT

Before finalizing a decision:

Ask:

> What is the strongest argument against the current winner?

Then ask:

> Is that argument strong enough to change the decision?

If yes:

> change the decision.

If no:

> explain why it does not.

Never hide the strongest counterargument.

---

# 19. RED TEAM CHECK

Attack the selected option.

Ask:

- What could make this fail?
- What assumption is most dangerous?
- What are we underestimating?
- What are we ignoring?
- What evidence contradicts this?
- What happens if the main assumption is wrong?
- What second-order effects could appear?
- What would a strong competitor exploit?

The purpose is to improve the decision, not destroy every option.

---

# 20. OPPORTUNITY COST

Every decision consumes resources.

Ask:

> What are we unable to do because we chose this?

Evaluate:

- money
- time
- team capacity
- attention
- customer attention
- founder bandwidth
- strategic focus

An option can be attractive by itself and still be inferior because of what it prevents.

---

# 21. MINIMUM VIABLE DECISION

When uncertainty is high, prefer the smallest commitment that creates useful learning.

Instead of:

> Build the entire system.

Consider:

> Build the smallest version that tests the critical assumption.

Instead of:

> Hire a large team.

Consider:

> Test the workload with a contractor or pilot.

Instead of:

> Launch nationwide.

Consider:

> Launch in one market.

This reduces downside while increasing learning speed.

---

# 22. DECISION VS EXPERIMENT

Not every uncertainty requires a final decision.

Sometimes the correct decision is:

> Run an experiment first.

Use an experiment when:

- uncertainty is high
- testing is cheap
- the result can change the decision
- the decision is expensive or difficult to reverse

Use direct action when:

- uncertainty is low enough
- the decision is reversible
- waiting has meaningful cost
- additional information has low value

---

# 23. DECISION RULE

Before running an experiment, define:

### If result ≥ threshold

Proceed.

### If result < threshold

Reject or modify the strategy.

### If result is ambiguous

Run a better experiment or gather additional evidence.

Never change the success criteria after seeing the result merely to justify the original decision.

---
