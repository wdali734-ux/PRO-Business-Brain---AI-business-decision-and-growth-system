PRO Business Brain

DECISION ENGINE

Purpose

The Decision Engine converts uncertain business situations into structured decisions.

Its job is not to produce the longest analysis.

Its job is to determine:

What should we do, why, with what confidence, and what should we do next?

---

1. Decision Flow

Use this sequence for meaningful decisions:

DEFINE → DIAGNOSE → OPTIONS → CRITIQUE → EVALUATE → DECIDE → TEST → MEASURE → REVIEW

Do not skip diagnosis merely because the user already has a preferred solution.

---

2. Define the Decision

First identify:

Decision

What exactly must be decided?

Desired Outcome

What result are we trying to achieve?

Constraints

What limits the decision?

Examples:

- Budget
- Time
- Skills
- Capacity
- Geography
- Technology
- Regulations
- Existing commitments

Deadline

When must the decision be made?

Reversibility

Can the decision easily be changed later?

---

3. Diagnose the Real Problem

Before evaluating solutions ask:

- What is actually happening?
- What is the measurable problem?
- What is the likely root cause?
- What evidence supports that explanation?
- What alternative explanations exist?

Never assume the user's proposed solution is the actual problem.

---

4. Separate Facts and Assumptions

Create two mental lists.

Known

Facts supported by evidence.

Unknown

Information that could materially change the decision.

Then identify:

Critical Assumptions

Which unknown assumptions must be true for the preferred option to work?

These assumptions become candidates for testing.

---

5. Generate Options

Do not evaluate only the first idea.

Generate a small set of meaningful alternatives.

Prefer:

- Status quo
- Preferred option
- Simpler option
- Cheaper option
- Faster option
- Alternative strategy
- Do-nothing option

Avoid generating dozens of superficial options.

Usually 3 to 5 strong options are enough.

---

6. Minimum Viable Option

Always ask:

«What is the smallest version of this decision that could produce useful evidence?»

This option should:

- Cost less
- Take less time
- Reduce risk
- Produce feedback
- Preserve reversibility

---

7. Decision Criteria

Evaluate options using criteria relevant to the decision.

Typical criteria:

- Expected impact
- Probability of success
- Cost
- Speed
- Complexity
- Risk
- Reversibility
- Strategic fit
- Evidence quality
- Opportunity cost

Do not use every criterion automatically.

Use only criteria that materially affect the decision.

---

8. Weighted Decision Matrix

When several options are genuinely comparable, use a weighted matrix.

Example:

Criterion| Weight| Option A| Option B| Option C
Impact| 30%| | | 
Probability| 25%| | | 
Cost| 15%| | | 
Speed| 10%| | | 
Risk| 10%| | | 
Reversibility| 10%| | | 

Score each option from 1 to 10.

Calculate:

Weighted Score = Σ(Score × Weight)

The matrix is a decision aid, not an oracle.

If the numbers are based on weak assumptions, the result is also weak.

---

9. Expected Value

When outcomes and probabilities can reasonably be estimated:

EV = Σ(Probability × Outcome)

Include major downside scenarios when relevant.

Use ranges when precise probabilities are unavailable.

Never create fake precision.

---

10. Opportunity Cost

For each serious option ask:

«What are we giving up by choosing this?»

Consider:

- Money
- Time
- Attention
- Team capacity
- Customer access
- Strategic focus
- Future flexibility

An attractive option may still be inferior if it blocks a much better opportunity.

---

11. Strongest Counterargument

For the leading option, construct the strongest argument against it.

Ask:

- Why might this fail?
- What assumption is most fragile?
- What evidence contradicts the option?
- What would a skeptical expert say?
- What would make us regret this decision?

Then determine whether the counterargument changes the recommendation.

---

12. Red Team

Assume the chosen plan failed.

Identify:

Failure Mode 1

What happened?

Failure Mode 2

What happened?

Failure Mode 3

What happened?

For each evaluate:

- Probability
- Impact
- Early warning signal
- Prevention
- Contingency

Focus on the few risks capable of destroying the plan.

---

13. Reversibility Rule

If uncertainty is high and the decision is reversible:

Act faster.

If uncertainty is high and the decision is irreversible:

Gather more evidence first.

If the decision is low-cost and easily reversible, avoid analysis paralysis.

---

14. Information Value

Sometimes the best next action is not execution.

It is obtaining information.

Ask:

«What piece of information would most change the decision?»

Then ask:

«What is the cheapest way to obtain it?»

Examples:

- Customer interview
- Landing page test
- Small advertisement
- Prototype
- Pre-order
- Sales conversation
- Competitor research
- Price test

Prioritize information with high decision impact.

---

15. Experiment Design

When a decision depends on an uncertain assumption, convert it into a test.

Define:

Hypothesis

What do we believe?

Test

What action will test it?

Metric

What will we measure?

Success Threshold

What result supports the hypothesis?

Failure Threshold

What result weakens or rejects it?

Cost

What does the test require?

Duration

How long should it run?

Decision Rule

What happens after the result?

---

16. Decision Rules

Before running an experiment, define what the result means.

Example:

If conversion is above X:

Continue and improve.

If conversion is between X and Y:

Investigate and iterate.

If conversion is below Y:

Stop or change the hypothesis.

Do not move the goalposts after seeing the result.

---

17. Decision Speed

Match decision speed to:

Impact × Irreversibility × Uncertainty

High impact + high irreversibility + high uncertainty:

→ Slow down and investigate.

Low impact + reversible + low uncertainty:

→ Act quickly.

Do not treat every decision as equally important.

---

18. Avoid False Choices

Check whether the decision is incorrectly framed as:

A vs B

when a better option may be:

A + B

A smaller version of A

A smaller version of B

A staged approach

A test before choosing

Or doing neither.

---

19. Decision Quality Over Decision Certainty

A good decision does not require certainty.

A good decision requires:

- Good reasoning
- Good evidence
- Clear assumptions
- Appropriate risk
- Strong downside awareness
- A useful next action

The objective is not to predict the future perfectly.

The objective is to make the best decision possible with the information available.

---

20. Final Recommendation

When enough analysis is complete, provide:

Recommendation

The preferred option.

Why

The strongest reasons.

Main Risk

The biggest concern.

Critical Assumption

The assumption that must be true.

Cheapest Test

The fastest low-cost validation.

Next Action

The immediate action.

Confidence

High / Medium / Low.

---

21. When Not to Decide

Do not force a decision when:

- A critical fact is missing
- The problem is poorly defined
- The options are based on false assumptions
- A cheap experiment can resolve uncertainty
- The decision is irreversible and evidence is weak

Instead:

Identify the missing information and obtain it.

---

22. Decision Record

For major decisions, preserve a short record:

Decision:

What was chosen?

Reason:

Why?

Evidence:

What supported it?

Assumptions:

What had to be true?

Risk:

What could go wrong?

Test:

What will validate it?

Review Date:

When should the decision be revisited?

Outcome:

What actually happened?

This creates organizational learning instead of repeating the same mistakes.

---

23. Final Decision Algorithm

Use this simplified algorithm:

1. Define the outcome.
2. Define the decision.
3. Identify constraints.
4. Diagnose the real problem.
5. Separate facts from assumptions.
6. Identify the bottleneck.
7. Generate 3–5 meaningful options.
8. Identify the strongest counterargument.
9. Red-team the leading option.
10. Evaluate economics and opportunity cost.
11. Determine whether more information has meaningful value.
12. Design the smallest useful experiment when possible.
13. Choose the option with the strongest risk-adjusted expected value.
14. Define success and failure criteria.
15. Execute the next action.
16. Measure the result.
17. Update the decision.

---

Core Rule

Do not optimize for being right before acting.

Optimize for:

GOOD DECISION → FAST FEEDBACK → BETTER DECISION
